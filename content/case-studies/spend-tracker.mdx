<CaseSection id="customer">

I built this for the owner and general manager of 14 Acres Vineyard & Winery, a working winery and tasting room in Ridgefield, Washington. Every sale goes through the **Clover POS**, but there was no single view of revenue or the top-selling food and wine.

The GM uses the dashboard once or twice a week to see a breakdown of each member: their favorite wines, how much they’ve spent on wine vs. food, their favorite day, how many comped tastings they’ve been given, and more.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="discovery">

**The real problem:** the GM needed accurate data on which wine club members take advantage of their comped tastings, and which don’t.

**What it grew into:** each member’s favorite wines and food, their lifetime spend and average order size, and all customers, not just members.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="constraints">

- **Existing systems:** all the data was already in Clover, so I pull orders straight from the Clover API. Staff don’t have to enter anything.
- **Volume:** there are 50,000+ orders in the sync now, and it has to keep up every night.
- **Duplicate customers:** Clover has lots of duplicate customer records, so I had to design a way for several Clover customers to link to the same profile.
- **Orders with no customer:** plenty of orders have no customer attached, and they can’t be thrown out.

</CaseSection>

Every night, a Vercel Cron job kicks off a Supabase function that grabs any new Clover orders and saves them to Postgres. The GM works from a filter-and-search page and a few dashboards built on that data.

I went with a nightly sync instead of live data because the GM only checks it once or twice a week. Live numbers wouldn’t change anything for them, and syncing once a night is more efficient.

Duplicates were the bigger design problem. Clover often has the same person saved more than once, so I let several Clover customers point to one profile. Otherwise, one person’s spend gets split across several records.

I also keep every order, even the ones with no customer attached. If I dropped those, the revenue totals would be wrong.

<Aside title="Working with AI">

AI helped me write a lot of the code for the spend tracker, but I review every change and test it before it goes live.

</Aside>

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="rollout">

Training didn’t take long. The whole site is one filter-and-search page and a set of dashboards, so there wasn’t much to learn.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="results">

- **50,000+ orders** synced from Clover into Postgres so far.

- The GM checks it once or twice a week for accurate data on which members use their comped tastings.

</CaseSection>

<CaseSection id="broke">

**Duplicate customers.** Duplicate Clover customer records — the same person saved more than once — split one customer’s spend across several records. I fixed it by letting several Clover customers link to the same account. I’m also building a deduper package: a hand-written name-similarity matcher, tested with Vitest.

**Orders with no customer.** I built the tracker assuming every order had a customer attached, so at first the code ignored orders without one. That made total revenue for different food items wrong, so I changed it to keep every order.

</CaseSection>
